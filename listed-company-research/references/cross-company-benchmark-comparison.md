# Cross-Company Benchmark Comparison Framework

When the user frames a new research target in reference to a previously-analyzed company's success pattern (e.g., "宇通成功了，中材国际是不是也跨过了同样的拐点？"), use this framework to rigorously test whether the superficial similarity holds up under scrutiny.

## When to Load This Reference

- User says "顺着X公司的经验看看Y公司"
- User frames a target as "the next [previously analyzed company]"
- Two companies share a surface metric (e.g., "both have overseas revenue > 50%")
- User asks "is Company B the same story as Company A?"

## The Three-Test Framework

### Test 1: Trajectory Verification — Organic Second Curve vs U-Shape Reversion

The most common trap: two companies arrive at the same headline metric (e.g., "overseas > 50%") via completely different paths. Plot the overseas revenue % over 10+ years for both companies side by side.

**True second curve (宇通 pattern):** Starts low (11-14%), grows organically over a decade to exceed 50%. The海外 business was built from scratch — every percentage point gained was earned through new market entry, product development, and network building.

**U-shape reversion (中材 pattern):** Started high (82%), dropped to a trough (37%), then recovered to 55%. The "crossing" of the 50% line is a return to historical norms, not a new growth trajectory. Often caused by:
- A one-time domestic revenue surge (M&A consolidation, policy-driven boom)
- Overseas revenue remaining flat while domestic inflated the denominator
- Then domestic shrinking back, passively restoring overseas share

**Key diagnostic:** Calculate overseas revenue **absolute value** trend, not just the ratio. If overseas absolute revenue grew from 36亿 to 211亿 (宇通), that's a real second curve. If it went from 186亿 to 272亿 over 10 years (中材), that's marginal growth that barely outpaces inflation — the "crossing" was driven by domestic contraction, not overseas acceleration.

### Test 2: Margin Spread — Does Overseas Earn a Premium?

The single most decisive metric for comparing two "globalizing" companies:

| Metric | True premium globalizer | Structural commodity player |
|--------|------------------------|---------------------------|
| Overseas margin vs domestic | **>5 percentage points** | **<3 percentage points** |
| Implication | Brand/tech premium creates profit leverage | No pricing power; wins on cost/execution only |

**宇通 example:** Overseas margin 29.6% vs domestic 19.1% = **10.5pp spread**. Every overseas unit sold earns 1.55x the domestic unit's gross profit. This is a brand-driven pricing premium — customers pay more for the technology gap (new energy) and service network.

**中材 example:** Overseas margin 18.9% vs domestic 17.4% = **1.5pp spread**. Overseas projects don't command a meaningful premium. This reflects the EPC/project-bidding business model — there's no "product premium" because each project is competitively tendered. The customer (cement plant owner) cares about cost and schedule, not brand.

**Why this matters more than overseas %:** A company with 40% overseas at a 10pp margin premium is worth more than one with 55% overseas at a 1.5pp spread. The profit leverage from overseas depends on the margin gap, not the revenue ratio.

### Test 3: Business Model Compatibility

Verify that the "success formula" from Company A is even transferable to Company B's business model:

| Dimension | Product company (宇通) | Project/engineering company (中材) |
|-----------|----------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Revenue model | Standardized product sale (repeatable) | Project-based (one-off per contract) |
| Customer lock-in | Parts/service/upgrades create recurring revenue | Next project requires re-bidding |
| Order visibility | Contract liability (prepayments) — short cycle (~6-12 months) | Backlog of signed contracts — long cycle (2-3 years per project) |
| Working capital | Low AR (~13% of revenue), fast cash conversion | High AR+contract assets (~48% of revenue), slow cash conversion |
| Scalability | Manufacturing scale = cost advantage compounding | Each project = standalone cost center |
| Margin expansion path | Product premium from brand + tech gap | Execution efficiency (hard to scale beyond cost discipline) |

A success formula built on "product premium + service network lock-in" cannot transfer to a project-bidding business model. The moat mechanisms are fundamentally different.

## Output Format

Present the comparison as a **conclusion-first** section at the top of the report (before diving into company fundamentals), structured as:

1. **One-sentence conclusion:** "Using [Company A]'s framework to look at [Company B], the surface similarity is [real/misleading] because [key reason]."
2. **Trajectory table:** Side-by-side overseas % over 10+ years, with the different shapes annotated
3. **Margin spread comparison:** The decisive test — show both companies' overseas vs domestic margins side by side
4. **Business model compatibility check:** Why Company A's moat may or may not apply to Company B
5. **Investment thesis translation:** "If you liked Company A for [reason], the equivalent logic for Company B is [different reason / not applicable]"

## Session Case Study: 宇通客车 vs 中材国际 (2026-06-19)

**Surface trigger:** Both crossed "海外 > 国内" in 2024-2025.

**Test 1 result:** Different trajectories. 宇通: 13%→51% (true second curve). 中材: 82%→37%→55% (U-shape reversion driven by domestic contraction, not海外 acceleration).

**Test 2 result:** Decisive difference. 宇通海外 margin premium = 10.5pp (real brand premium). 中材海外 margin premium = 1.5pp (project-bidding parity).

**Test 3 result:** Incompatible models. 宇通's "product → tech output → service network" moat doesn't transfer to中材's EPC project model. 中材 has one advantage宇通 lacks: order backlog visibility (665亿结转合同 = 1.34x annual revenue).

**Conclusion delivered to user:** "不能用'第二个宇通'的思路来理解中材国际" — the two companies share a headline metric but not the underlying economic dynamics. 宇通 is a growth stock (high ROE 38%, accelerating profit); 中材 is a deep-value/CPE-reform play (low PE 8x, PB 0.97x breaking net asset value, but declining margins and AR risk).
